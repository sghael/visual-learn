Vision Transformers need registers

Big ViTs quietly hijack a few boring background patches as scratch memory. That produces spiky, high-norm artifact tokens and noisy attention maps. Giving the model a few blank register tokens instead cleans it up. Scroll to see how.

Based on Darcet, Oquab, Mairal & Bojanowski (ICLR 2024) Figures use a simulated model
01 · Recap

An image becomes a sequence of tokens

A Vision Transformer (ViT) cuts the image into a grid of fixed-size patches, usually 14 or 16 pixels on a side. Each patch is flattened and linearly projected into a vector, then a positional embedding is added. Those vectors are the patch tokens.

One extra learned token, [CLS], is prepended. It has no pixels of its own. Its job is to gather a summary of the whole image through attention, and its final state is what gets used for classification.

The sequence then runs through a stack of identical transformer blocks. At every layer, every token attends to every other token. Nothing in the architecture says a patch token has to keep describing its patch. It is just a slot in the sequence.

02 · The problem

Some tokens go rogue in the middle of the network

If you look at the attention map of a modern self-supervised ViT like DINOv2, you expect it to light up the object. Instead the map is peppered with a few bright spots in flat, featureless regions: sky, wall, road. These spots come from artifact tokens whose embedding norm is roughly ten times larger than their neighbours'.

03 · Hypothesis

The model is recycling redundant tokens as scratch space

Put the clues together:

They appear in redundant patches. An artifact token almost always sits in a patch whose embedding was nearly identical to its neighbours' just before the switch. A sky patch surrounded by sky carries no information the network could not recover from next door.

They drop their local content and pick up global content. The token stops encoding where it is or what it looked like, and starts encoding what the image is.

They only show up in big models trained long enough. Small models do not have spare capacity to spend on the trick; large ones learn it during training.

The interpretation: the transformer needs somewhere to store and mix global information while it processes the image, and the only slots it has are tokens. So it overwrites the least useful ones. The high norm is a side effect: an easy way to make a token dominate softmax attention so other tokens can read from it.

That is fine for classification. It is bad for anything that reads patch tokens directly: segmentation, depth, object discovery, and any attention-map visualization.

04 · The fix

Give it scratch space on purpose: register tokens

If the model wants extra slots, hand them over. A register is just another learned token, exactly like [CLS], appended to the sequence after the patch tokens. Registers have no pixels, no position in the image, and no loss attached to them. They participate in every attention layer, and at the output they are simply thrown away.

With even one register available, the model stops overwriting patches. The high norms move into the registers, where nobody is reading them, and the patch tokens keep describing their patches. Slide the register count below and watch the norm map and the attention map.

Why it works. The artifacts were never a bug in attention. They were a workaround for a missing feature: a place to think that is not an image patch. Registers are that place. Once they exist, the cheapest solution for the optimizer is to use them.
05 · Evidence

What changes when you add four registers

The authors retrained three very different ViT-L models with four registers: DeiT-III (supervised), OpenCLIP (text-supervised) and DINOv2 (self-supervised). In all three the artifacts disappear and the attention maps become clean.

Classification barely moves, which makes sense because [CLS] was already getting what it needed. Dense tasks that read the patch tokens improve, and the unsupervised object-discovery method LOST, which relies on patch-to-patch similarity, improves dramatically on DINOv2.

06 · Details

Questions people ask next

How many registers do I need?
One is enough to remove the artifacts. The paper uses four by default because dense-task performance keeps creeping up to about four and then flattens. Beyond that you pay sequence length for nothing.
Where do they go in the sequence?
After the patch tokens. Position does not matter to attention, but putting them at the end keeps the [CLS]-first, patches-next convention that downstream code assumes. They get no positional embedding.
Are registers the same as [CLS]?
Mechanically yes: a learned vector, prepended or appended, updated by every layer. The difference is intent. [CLS] is read at the output and has a loss attached; registers are never read and have no loss. Interestingly, registers end up specializing: different registers attend to different parts of the object.
Is this the same as attention sinks in language models?
Closely related. LLMs also grow a few massive-activation tokens, usually the first token, that soak up attention mass. StreamingLLM's "attention sink" tokens and the "massive activations" line of work describe the same pressure: softmax needs somewhere to dump probability, and the model needs somewhere to keep global state. Registers are the vision-side answer.
Can I add registers to a model I already have?
Not for free. The artifacts are learned behaviour; a frozen model will keep producing them and ignore new tokens. You need to fine-tune with the registers present. Training from scratch is what the paper did. Later work explores test-time and training-free variants, but the clean result needs training.
Why did small models not have artifacts?
The paper observes artifacts in ViT-B and larger and after enough training. A plausible reading is capacity: a small model cannot afford to sacrifice patch information, and the global-scratch trick only pays off once the model is big enough to exploit it.
