Chapter 1

Two ways to multiply a matrix

Almost everything a neural network does is a matrix multiply. GPUs and TPUs are two different answers to the question "how do you do billions of those per second?", and every spec sheet difference flows from that choice.

GPU: thousands of small cores, scheduled

  • A die is tiled into streaming multiprocessors (SMs). An H100 has 132; a B200 has two dies with 148 across them.
  • Each SM runs warps of 32 threads in lockstep and contains Tensor Cores, small fixed-function matrix units.
  • Work is scheduled dynamically. That flexibility is why GPUs run CUDA, graphics, simulation and ML equally well.
  • Memory: HBM3e on data-center parts, GDDR on the L4 and RTX PRO 6000.

TPU: one giant systolic array, streamed

  • The heart is the MXU, a grid of multiply-accumulators. v5 used 128×128; Trillium and Ironwood use 256×256, so one MXU does 65,536 MACs per clock.
  • Inputs flow in from the edges and partial sums ripple through the grid. No instruction fetch, no scheduler, very little wasted energy.
  • The compiler (XLA) must lay out the whole computation ahead of time. Great for transformers, less flexible for irregular code.
  • Each chip also has SparseCores for embeddings and vector units for everything that is not a matmul.

Chapter 3

Memory vs. compute, on one map

Put every chip on the same two axes and the generations line up as a diagonal march toward the top right. The interesting chips are the ones off the diagonal.

Peak dense throughput against memory per chip
Both axes are logarithmic. Throughput is NVIDIA's or Google's published peak dense figure (no 2:1 sparsity), which is what you can actually reach. Chips without native FP8 (A100, V100, T4) are plotted at their INT8 or FP16 peak. Older N1-attachable GPUs are shown at their FP16 tensor peak.

What to see. The v5e and L4 sit in the bottom-left corner: small memory, modest compute, and by far the cheapest per hour. The Blackwell parts and Ironwood share the top-right: around 180 to 280 GB and 4 to 5 PFLOPS of FP8 per chip. The H200 and Ironwood are the two chips with far more memory than their compute alone would suggest, which is exactly what you want for serving long-context models where the KV cache, not the weights, fills the memory.

Chapter 4

Where the bottleneck is

A chip has two speed limits: how fast it can compute, and how fast it can pull bytes from memory. Which one you hit depends on your workload's arithmetic intensity, the number of floating-point operations per byte loaded. The roofline model draws both limits on one chart.

Decode with batch 64 ≈ 64 FLOP/byte. Drag left toward batch-1 decoding, right toward training.
Ridge point = peak FLOPS ÷ memory bandwidth. Left of it, the chip is starved for bytes; right of it, starved for math. LLM decoding at small batch loads every weight once per token and does about 2 FLOPs per weight, so it sits far to the left on every chip in this atlas. That is why inference chips chase bandwidth and capacity (H200, Ironwood) rather than raw FLOPS.
Chapter 5

How chips talk to each other

A single chip trains nothing interesting. The real product is the fabric: how many chips share a fast interconnect before you fall back to Ethernet. NVIDIA and Google draw that boundary in very different places.

NVLink islands. On A3 and A4, eight GPUs in one VM share an NVSwitch at 900 GB/s (Hopper) or 1.8 TB/s (Blackwell) per GPU. Anything bigger goes over the VM's network: RoCE RDMA at up to 3.6 Tbps on A3 Ultra and A4, or GPUDirect-TCPXO at 4.8 Tbps on A3 Mega. The A4X series stretches the island to 72 GPUs across 18 VMs in one NVL72 rack, so a 70B model's tensor-parallel group never leaves NVLink.

TPU tori. Each TPU chip has direct ICI links to its neighbours in a 2D (v5e, v6e) or 3D (v5p, Ironwood) torus; the whole pod is one fabric. Google's optical circuit switches can carve a 9,216-chip Ironwood pod into any rectangular slice you ask for, and route around failed racks. There is no "island" boundary at 8 or 72; the cost is that communication patterns must be torus-friendly, which XLA handles for you.

Chapter 7

How you actually buy it

The same chip can cost a very different amount, and be very differently available, depending on how you ask for it. The frontier parts are not on the on-demand shelf at all.

On demand

Pay per second, stop when you like. Reliable for G2, G4, A2, A3 High and the older TPUs; often capacity-limited for anything newer.

L4 · RTX PRO 6000 · A100 · H100 · v5e · v5p · v6e · Ironwood

Spot

60 to 91% cheaper, pre-emptible with 30 seconds' notice. Ideal for checkpointed training, batch inference and evaluation runs.

most GPUs · all TPUs

Dynamic Workload Scheduler

Flex-start queues your job and runs it for up to 7 days when capacity frees, at a discount. Calendar mode books a future block of 1 to 90 days.

A3 · A4 · A4X · TPUs via GKE or MIGs

Reservations and CUDs

Capacity you own for a term. The only way to get GB200, GB300 and full-pod TPU slices; 1- and 3-year commitments cut the rate by 30 to 55%.

Chapter 8

Glossary

HBM
High Bandwidth Memory, stacked DRAM sitting next to the die. Capacity sets what fits; bandwidth sets decode speed.
NVLink / NVSwitch
NVIDIA's GPU-to-GPU fabric inside a server (8 GPUs) or, with NVL72, a rack (72 GPUs).
ICI
Inter-Chip Interconnect, Google's TPU-to-TPU links, wired as a torus and reconfigured by optical circuit switches.
RoCE / GPUDirect
RDMA over Ethernet between VMs. On A3 Ultra, A4 and A4X it runs on Google's Titanium offload NICs.
Dense vs. sparse FLOPS
NVIDIA quotes 2:1 structured-sparsity peaks that double the dense number. This atlas uses dense everywhere.
MXU
Matrix Multiply Unit, the systolic array at the core of a TPU TensorCore.
SparseCore
A TPU co-processor for embedding lookups and other gather-scatter work; four per chip on v5p and Ironwood.
Slice
A rectangular sub-block of a TPU pod (for example 4×4×4 = 64 Ironwood chips) rented as one unit.
Multislice
Running one job across several slices joined over the data-center network.
vWS
Virtual workstation licence variant of a GPU (L4, RTX PRO 6000, T4, P4) for remote graphics.
Arithmetic intensity
FLOPs per byte moved. Low for decoding, high for training and prefill.
