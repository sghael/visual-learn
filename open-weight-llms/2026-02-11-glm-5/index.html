GLM-5

How an agent can learn while other agents are still working

A training batch of agents does not finish like a synchronized classroom exam. One task may need a quick calculation while another waits for a long software build. GLM-5 separates experience generation from learning so that the slowest task does not always hold the entire system idle.

The slowest rollout can determine the next update

A rollout is one sampled attempt at a task, including the model’s actions and the environment’s replies. In a simple synchronous scheme, a collection of rollouts must finish before the learner updates. Different task lengths then create waiting time.

Consider three rollout workers taking 2, 3 and 10 time units. If all wait for a barrier at time 10, the first worker sits idle for 8 units and the second for 7. Their spare time is a scheduling problem; it does not imply that the long task is less valuable.

GLM-5’s report describes separate inference and training engines. The inference side continually produces trajectories, while a learner consumes completed batches and periodically sends updated weights back. Task-specific services provide different environments through a common orchestration layer.

[1]

Overlap reduces waiting, not the amount of work

The interactive schedule below uses four independent jobs taking 3, 2, 4 and 1 arbitrary time units. With one worker, the total is 10. Adding workers overlaps tasks. It is a small analogy for keeping workers busy, not a simulation of the GLM training system.

Watch the longest worker row. That row determines the completion time of this finite job set. If every job depended on the previous result, additional workers would have no effect. The arithmetic therefore separates available parallelism from the number of machines purchased.

Training adds a further complication: a completed rollout may have been generated using older weights than the learner now holds. Faster scheduling changes the statistical problem as well as the hardware utilization. A useful system must account for both.

Old experience came from an older policy

A policy is the model’s distribution over possible actions. After learning, that distribution changes. If a worker produced a trajectory before the latest updates, the learner cannot pretend it came from the current policy. This mismatch is commonly called policy lag or off-policy data.

GLM-5 discusses periodic synchronization, asynchronous optimization safeguards, and preserving exact generated token IDs. Reconstructing a rollout by re-tokenizing its displayed text can change boundaries and misalign the actions whose probabilities are used during learning.

[1]

For an analogy, a coach reviews a runner’s lap recorded before a change in technique. The lap is useful evidence, but it should be labeled with the technique actually used. Erasing that label makes it difficult to know which change deserves credit. In language-model training, the labels include token identities and behavior-policy information.

This is why asynchronous learning is more than moving a queue between two programs. The data must retain enough information for the objective to remain meaningful when generation and optimization no longer share one clock.

Reduce reading cost separately from training waits

GLM-5 also adopts DeepSeek Sparse Attention and scales to 744 billion total parameters with 40 billion active. Sparse attention chooses a subset of earlier context for expensive attention operations. This is inherited from DeepSeek work; the asynchronous learning system addresses a different bottleneck.

[2]

A sparse expert selects which parameter networks process a token. Sparse attention selects which earlier token representations to consult. Neither automatically fixes idle rollout workers. Keeping these mechanisms separate makes efficiency claims interpretable.

Later GLM-5.2 introduced IndexShare, reusing an indexer across groups of sparse-attention layers, while GLM-5.3 reused the 5.2 base and emphasized post-training. GLM-5.3-Flash then changed the base architecture again. The official family repository separates these developments.

[3]

The broader lesson is to identify the cost before choosing the remedy: stored parameters, context retrieval, time waiting for tools and stale training data are different engineering problems.

What this tutorial leaves out

  • The scheduling widget illustrates independent job overlap; it does not reproduce slime or asynchronous policy optimization.
  • Efficiency gains from sparse attention and asynchronous RL cannot be multiplied into an overall speedup without measurements.
